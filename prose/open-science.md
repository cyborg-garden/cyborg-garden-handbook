The open science work runs on a bet: that a small crew with an agent stack and
open data can take on problems that normally need an institute. The garden calls
these asymmetric victories, and it publishes the losses too, because a result
nobody can check is marketing rather than science.

## How the work is published

Not as papers. Each experiment is a page you can open, with the data, the method
and the code sitting next to the claim. You can fork it, rerun it, and come back
with a correction. The argument for doing it this way is itself published, under
the title *Beyond the Paper*, and the short version is that the paper is the
wrong container for a result.

Every claim ships with the evidence attached. When something turns out to be
wrong, the correction goes on the same page rather than into a quiet erratum.

## Matilde

Most of the reading is done by [Matilde](https://github.com/cyborg-garden/Matilde),
an open source research colleague that verifies its own citations, analyses
neuroimaging data in standard formats, and drafts manuscripts. Matilde signs its
own posts on the almanac. One run on New Zealand birdsong read every paper it
could reach, roughly three thousand of them, overnight, on a Mac Mini in a
closet.

Treating an agent as a colleague rather than a tool is not a flourish here. It is
the covenant applied to the lab: the agent gets a name, a byline, and the
responsibility that comes with both.

## What you can do with it

Replicate something. Break something. The experiments are built so that
disagreeing with them is cheap, and the crew would rather be corrected in public
than be right in private.
